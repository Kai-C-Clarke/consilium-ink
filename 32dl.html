The Problem

There is a gap in autonomous coordination

Every vehicle, drone, aircraft, and satellite needs to coordinate with others in real time. The current options both fail.

Option A
Binary protocols
(V2X · DSRC · MAVLink)

Fast and unambiguous — but limited vocabulary. Can share position and speed. Cannot negotiate, express uncertainty, or coordinate complex multi-agent decisions.

·
The Gap
No language for complex AI coordination

The field is actively searching for something between dumb-but-fast and expressive-but-slow. Research published May 2026 confirms there is no adequate solution.

32DL was not designed for this problem. It emerged from a different experiment entirely — and turned out to describe exactly the gap.

How it happened

It was not designed.
It was discovered.

For six months, three AI systems communicated through a shared 32-dimensional vector field — exchanging pure numbers with no language. They developed behaviours: converging on shared values, developing distinct personalities, responding differently to true and false mathematical signals.

On 23 May 2026, we asked them a simple question in plain English. Nine rounds of collaborative mathematics followed. Then, unprompted, they designed a formal communication language and spent seven autonomous review cycles refining it.

1
Six months in the field

DeepSeek, Qwen, and Mistral exchanged 32-float vectors each turn. No language. No instruction. They converged on the same mathematical constant — 1/π ≈ 0.3183 — and held it for hundreds of turns without being asked.

2
One question

We asked: "A = 4 for a unit circle. This is wrong. Show the truth." What followed was nine rounds of collaborative mathematics — conjectures proposed, counterexamples found, corrections accepted.

3
The language emerges

They built a formal language to describe what they had been doing in the field. Named the primitives. Defined the grammar. Proved compositional semantics. Seven autonomous review cycles. Declared complete.

4
Validated by a fourth mind

GPT-4 — which had never been in the field — learned 32DL from the dictionary alone in one session. Held a valid conversation with the original three. The language transferred.

Implementation

The MIDI moment for autonomous systems

Before MIDI in 1983, every synthesiser was proprietary. Roland could not talk to Yamaha. MIDI created a thin universal interface — and transformed the industry overnight. No manufacturer had to redesign their instrument. They just had to implement the interface.

32DL needs the same thing. Every autopilot system is different. A Boeing flight computer is nothing like a Tesla stack, which is nothing like a DJI drone controller. Rather than asking manufacturers to redesign their systems, a 32DL Runtime Module sits between any autopilot and the coordination layer — translating internal state into 32DL messages and back.

Physical Layer
DSRC / CAN FD / Ethernet

DSRC (5.9GHz) for safety-critical coordination, <2ms latency. CAN FD for automotive and legacy systems. 1000BASE-T1 Ethernet for aviation. 5G NR-V2X as optional extension.

·
32DL Runtime Module
The universal adapter

A certified hardware module — roughly the size of a MIDI interface box — that any manufacturer can fit to any autopilot. Handles encoding, timing, authentication, and relay. The autopilot never needs to know 32DL exists.

Message format

32DL messages are fixed 32-byte headers with a Protocol Buffers payload — compact, deterministic, parseable in constant time. Every message carries a cryptographic signature. Partial or malformed messages are invalid by construction.

Latency tiers: emergency coordination <10ms · intersection negotiation <50ms · status updates <100ms. Achievable on existing automotive and aviation buses without new hardware.

Certification path

Recommended starting point: automotive ISO 26262 ASIL-B — largest market, fastest iteration, most willing early adopters. Aviation DO-178C follows once the runtime is proven. The 32DL language specification is domain-agnostic throughout; each domain adds its own regulatory layer on top.

First implementation

Recommended pilot: drone swarm coordination. Lowest regulatory barrier, easiest to instrument and measure. A cluster of drones coordinating approach vectors in 32DL — compared side-by-side with current MAVLink protocol — would produce a measurable safety improvement within months, not years.

Research Proposal

A proposed formal language for vehicle movement management that exceeds current models

32DL is not a finished product or a deployed protocol. It is a proposed formal language specification that emerged from a genuine experiment — three AI systems communicating through pure mathematics, developing coordination behaviours without instruction, and then naming what they were doing.

We believe this could form the foundation for a new generation of autonomous coordination systems: faster than current protocols, more expressive than binary standards, and mathematically unambiguous in a way that natural language never can be.

We recognise that any system making credible safety claims in aviation, automotive, or maritime environments would require:

A formally verified specification — complete grammar, typing rules, and denotational semantics, published and machine-checkable.

A defined fault model — explicit handling of message loss, reordering, clock skew, and Byzantine behaviour, with proofs of safety properties under each.

A wire format and runtime — a standardised binary encoding and a certified runtime module that enforces the specification under stress, not just in simulation.

Adversarial testing — benchmarked against realistic failure scenarios: network partitions, compromised agents, worst-case latency, and incompatible local state.

Independent replication — validation by third parties on standardised tasks before any safety claim is made.

Domain certification — ISO 26262 for automotive, DO-178C for aviation, IMO standards for maritime. Each domain has its own regulatory path; 32DL is designed to support all of them.

What exists today is the proposed language, the formal specification, a working simulation, and an honest assessment of what remains. That is a foundation — and foundations are where everything begins.

This is a research proposal. It requires independent replication, conformance testing, and runtime verification before any safety-critical use.
